02 · USDA ERS meat price spreads¶

Milestone 1, item 1.3 · see docs/MILESTONES.md

What we're looking for¶

Notebook 01 showed how much beef, pork and chicken cost. This notebook gets the data that shows who gets the money. For every pound of meat sold at retail, USDA's Economic Research Service (ERS) estimates its value at three stages:

Stage What it means
Farm value What the rancher or hog farmer is paid for the animal
Wholesale value What the meatpacker gets for the cuts it sells to stores
Retail value What shoppers pay at the store

The gaps between them are the price spreads: farm-to-wholesale (roughly the packer's share) and wholesale-to-retail (roughly the grocery store's share). If beef's price rose 63% since 2015, the spreads tell us whether that money went to ranchers or stayed with packers and stores.

This notebook has two parts. Part 1 (this run) downloads the files and takes an inventory of what's in them. ERS splits the data across files with slightly different names for the same things, so before combining anything we'll review the inventory together and agree on which items to keep. Part 2 then combines them.

Steps (Part 1)

  1. Setup
  2. Which files we need, and why
  3. Download and save them, untouched, to data/raw/
  4. Load them and put them in one consistent shape
  5. Inventory: every data item, its units, and the months it covers

Step 2 · Which files we need, and why¶

The ERS Meat Price Spreads page offers five files. We need four of them, because the history and the recent months live in different files:

File Covers Why we need it
Historical monthly price spreads (beef, pork, broilers) 1970 to early 2026 (last updated Feb 2026) The long history, including 2015–2019
Choice beef values and spreads Last 24 months (Oct 2024 to Sep 2026) Brings beef up to date
Pork values and spreads Last 24 months Brings pork up to date
Summary of retail prices and price spreads Last 24 months The only current file with the chicken (broiler) spread

We skip the fifth file (retail prices by cut): we already have retail prices from BLS.

We download the CSV versions rather than Excel. They're already in a long, tidy layout (one row per item per month), which saves a lot of reshaping.

About chicken: ERS reports only the wholesale-to-retail spread for chicken, not a farm value. Most chickens are raised under contract for the processing companies rather than sold at a farm price, so there's no clean "farm value" to measure. Beef and pork get the full farm → wholesale → retail breakdown; chicken gets wholesale → retail only.

Step 3 · Download and save¶

Same rule as notebook 01: save exactly what ERS sent before changing anything. Each download goes into a dated folder, so a later re-download never overwrites this one.

Some government sites reject requests that don't look like they come from a browser, so we send a normal browser User-Agent header. If a download still fails, the error says which file, and you can download it by hand from the ERS page into the same folder.

Step 4 · Load and standardize¶

The four files are almost, but not quite, the same shape:

File Month columns
Historical Year, Month, Month-number
Summary Year, Month, Month_Number
Beef and pork Year, Period, Period_Number

The beef and pork files also mix annual and quarterly rows in with the monthly ones. Their Period_Number tells them apart: 1–12 are months, 13–16 are quarters, and 17 is the annual average. We keep months only.

ERS also writes values of 1,000 or more with a thousands comma (1,011.3). Python doesn't read that as a number, so the function strips the comma first. (The first run missed this: beef retail value, which passed $10.00/lb in late 2025, appeared to have 10 missing months. They weren't missing; they just had commas.)

The function below handles all three layouts and returns the same columns for every file: date, data_item, value, units, source_file.

In [10]:
def load_ers(path, source_name):
    """Load an ERS price-spread CSV and return monthly rows in one standard shape."""
    df = pd.read_csv(path, encoding="utf-8-sig")
    df.columns = [col.strip() for col in df.columns]

    # Find whichever month-number column this file uses
    month_col = next(col for col in ["Month-number", "Month_Number", "Period_Number"] if col in df.columns)

    df["month_num"] = pd.to_numeric(df[month_col], errors="coerce")
    df = df[df["month_num"].between(1, 12)].copy()   # drops quarters (13-16) and annual (17)

    df["date"] = pd.to_datetime(dict(year=df["Year"].astype(int), month=df["month_num"].astype(int), day=1))
    # Values of 1,000 or more come with a thousands comma ("1,011.3"); remove it first,
    # otherwise to_numeric turns those values into missing (NaN).
    df["value"] = pd.to_numeric(df["Value"].astype(str).str.replace(",", "", regex=False), errors="coerce")

    out = df.rename(columns={"Data_Item": "data_item", "Units": "units"})
    out["data_item"] = out["data_item"].str.strip()
    out["source_file"] = source_name
    return out[["date", "data_item", "value", "units", "source_file"]]

What it means¶

Reviewed together after the first run:

  • Together the files cover our whole period. The historical file ends in December 2025; the recent beef and pork files run October 2024 to September 2026. They overlap for 15 months, which lets us check they agree.
  • Same measures, different names. For example "byproduct value" (historical) vs "byproduct allowance" (recent). We'll match names by hand.
  • Chicken appears twice in the historical file because its units label changed in January 2025, from "Cents per retail pound" to "Cents per pound of retail equivalent". Part 2 checks whether that's only a relabel or a real change in method.
  • Chicken has a hole. The summary file only carries the latest 6 months of broiler data (April–September 2026), and the historical file stops at December 2025, so January–March 2026 is missing for chicken. That's longer than the 2-month limit in D18, so it stays a gap.
  • A first run showed beef retail value with only 14 of 24 recent months. That was a bug in our loader (values over 1,000 have a thousands comma), now fixed in Step 4.

Part 2 · Combine into one table¶

What we're looking for¶

One monthly table from January 2015 to September 2026 with, for each meat, the three values we agreed to keep (decision D22). "Three values" means the three stages of the supply chain, the same for each meat, not three data sources:

Value Meaning Meats
Retail value What shoppers pay Beef, pork, chicken
Wholesale value What the packer (or chicken processor) gets Beef, pork, chicken
Net farm value What the rancher or hog farmer gets, after subtracting hides and other byproducts Beef, pork

All in cents per pound of retail equivalent: the value per pound of meat as sold in the store, at each stage.

From those we calculate the spreads and shares ourselves, using ERS's definitions:

Calculated Formula Roughly whose share
Farm-to-wholesale spread wholesale − net farm Packer
Wholesale-to-retail spread retail − wholesale Grocery store
Farmer's share net farm ÷ retail Rancher / farmer

def pick(source, item):
    """Return one item from one file as a Series indexed by month (from 2015 on)."""
    df = frames[source]
    s = df[(df["data_item"] == item) & (df["date"] >= "2015-01-01")].set_index("date")["value"]
    return s.dropna().sort_index()

Step 7 · Do the files agree where they overlap?¶

What we're looking for: for the 15 months covered by both the historical and the recent files (October 2024 to December 2025), the values should be the same. If they differ a lot, ERS revised its numbers, and we'd need to decide which to trust.

overlap_rows = []
for meat, measure, hist_name, recent_file, recent_name in ITEM_MAP:
    if recent_file is None:

    r = pick(recent_file, recent_name)
    both = pd.concat([h, r], axis=1, keys=["historical", "recent"]).dropna()

Step 8 · Combine, preferring the newer files¶

For each meat and value we take the historical series and lay the recent series over it. Where both have a month, we use the recent file, because it was updated more recently (October 2026 vs February 2026) and would include any ERS revisions. Step 7 showed the choice makes no real difference, but it's the right default. A source column records where every number came from.

Step 9 · Chicken's recent wholesale value¶

For April–September 2026, the summary file gives chicken's retail value and its wholesale-to-retail spread. Since spread = retail − wholesale, it follows that:

wholesale = retail − spread

We check this works by applying the same formula to the historical file, where all three are reported, before using it on the recent months.

Step 10 · Calculate spreads and shares, and check them against ERS¶

We turn the table wide (one column per value) and calculate the spreads and the farmer's share. Then we compare our numbers with the spreads and shares ERS publishes. If they match, our join and our formulas are both right.

wide = values.pivot_table(index="date", columns=["meat", "measure"], values="value")

    retail = wide[(meat, "retail")]

    calc[(meat, "wholesale")] = wholesale
    calc[(meat, "spread_wholesale_to_retail")] = retail - wholesale

What we're looking for: whether our pork farm-to-wholesale spread is wrong, or ERS's published figure is. How to read it¶

Every value ERS reports for pork in October 2024, in cents per pound unless the units say percent.

What it means¶

  • ERS's farm-to-retail spread is 385.7 and its wholesale-to-retail spread is 305.8, so farm-to-wholesale should be 385.7 − 305.8 = 79.9 cents. That's exactly what we calculate (wholesale 186.2 − net farm 106.3 = 79.9).
  • ERS's published farm-to-wholesale figure is 16.2, which doesn't add up. But 16.2 is exactly the value of the row "farm-wholesale spread share of retail value", which is in percent (79.9 ÷ 492.0 = 16.2%).

Our calculated value is consistent with every other figure ERS publishes, so we keep ours (decision D23). The historical pork file doesn't have this problem (its check above matches to within 0.1).

This is a good example of why we calculate the spreads ourselves and check them, rather than copying the published columns.

What it means¶

Beef:

  • The retail value rose from about 629 cents/lb in 2015 to about 938 in 2025 ($6.29 to $9.38 per pound of retail beef, averaged across all cuts).
  • The farmer's share fell from about 52% in 2015 to 43% in 2019, then rose to about 54% in 2025, its highest of the three years. So in 2025 ranchers took a bigger slice of a much bigger dollar. That fits the drought → smaller herd → scarce cattle chain (D17): when cattle are scarce, the price of the animal itself is what rises.
  • The packer's share did the opposite: up to about 14% in 2019, down to under 5% in 2025. That's consistent with reports of packers paying more for cattle than they get for the meat, and with recent plant closures.
  • The store's share stayed close to 42% throughout.

Pork: much steadier. The farmer gets about 22–24% of the retail dollar, the packer about 15–16%, and the store about 60%, in all three years.

Chicken: the store's share fell slightly, from about 61% in 2015 to about 57% in 2025. That means the processor-and-farm side (the wholesale value) took a somewhat larger share of a retail price that rose from about $1.97 to $2.45 per pound.

These are first readings from yearly averages. Milestone 4 (item 4.2) looks at the monthly detail and tests them properly.
