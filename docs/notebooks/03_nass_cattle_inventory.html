03 · USDA NASS cattle inventory and cow slaughter¶

Milestone 1, items 1.4 and 1.5 · see docs/MILESTONES.md

What we're looking for¶

This notebook gets the data to test the cause chain in decision D17:

drought → less grazing → ranchers sell off cows → smaller herd → fewer cattle → higher beef prices

Two kinds of data from USDA's National Agricultural Statistics Service (NASS):

Data What it shows Link in the chain
Cattle inventory How many cattle and beef cows are on U.S. farms and ranches, counted each January 1 (and mid-year) "smaller herd"
Cow slaughter How many cows are slaughtered each month "ranchers sell off cows": a sell-off shows up here first, as a spike

Why cows specifically? Steers and heifers raised for beef are slaughtered every year as normal business. Cows are the breeding herd: every cow slaughtered is one fewer calf next year and the year after. A jump in cow slaughter means ranchers are shrinking their herds, which is exactly what happens in a drought.

Why start in 2010, not 2015? The cattle cycle is slow, so we need the years before 2015 for context. Ranchers sold off cows in the 2011–2013 drought (mainly Texas and the southern Plains), rebuilt the herd from about 2014 to 2019, then sold off again. Seeing the earlier cycle helps us judge whether the recent one is unusual (decision D24).

Like notebook 02, this has two parts. Part 1 downloads and takes an inventory: NASS has many similar-sounding series, so we'll choose which ones to use together before Part 2 builds the tables.

Steps (Part 1)

  1. Setup and API key
  2. The two queries
  3. Check the record counts (NASS limits each request to 50,000 rows)
  4. Download and save the raw responses

Step 2 · The two queries¶

NASS's Quick Stats API is searched with filters. Each filter narrows the results:

Filter Value Meaning
commodity_desc CATTLE Cattle data only
statisticcat_desc INVENTORY / SLAUGHTERED Head counts on farms, or animals slaughtered
agg_level_desc NATIONAL U.S. totals, not state or county
source_desc SURVEY NASS's regular surveys (not the 5-yearly Census of Agriculture)
year__GE 2010 Years greater than or equal to 2010
freq_desc MONTHLY Slaughter only: monthly figures

We don't narrow further (for example to "beef cows" only) yet, because we want to see every series NASS has before choosing.

Step 3 · Check the record counts¶

NASS refuses any request that would return more than 50,000 rows, so we ask how many rows each query would return before downloading. get_counts is a separate NASS endpoint that returns only the count.

In [10]:
def nass_get(endpoint, params):
    """Call a NASS Quick Stats endpoint and return the parsed JSON."""
    full = {"key": NASS_API_KEY, "format": "JSON", **params}
    resp = requests.get(f"{NASS_BASE}/{endpoint}/", params=full, timeout=120)
    if resp.status_code != 200:
        # NASS explains errors in the body; don't print resp.url, it contains the key
        raise RuntimeError(f"NASS {endpoint} failed ({resp.status_code}): {resp.text[:300]}")
    return resp.json()

for name, params in QUERIES.items():
    count = int(nass_get("get_counts", params)["count"])
    flag = "OK" if count <= 50_000 else "TOO MANY - narrow the query"
    print(f"{name:10} {count:>8,} rows  {flag}")

slaughter     5,226 rows  OK

Step 5 · Load into tables¶

Each NASS row has about 40 fields. We keep the ones that identify the series and its time period:

Field Meaning
short_desc The series name, e.g. CATTLE, COWS, BEEF - INVENTORY
year Year
reference_period_desc The period within the year: FIRST OF JAN, FIRST OF JUL, or a month (JAN, FEB…)
freq_desc ANNUAL, POINT IN TIME, MONTHLY…
Value The number, as text
unit_desc Units, e.g. HEAD

About Value: NASS writes numbers with thousands commas (28,700,000, the same problem as ERS) and uses codes for values it can't publish: (D) = withheld to protect individual operations, (NA) = not available, (Z) = less than half the unit shown. We strip the commas and turn the codes into missing values, keeping the original text in value_raw so nothing is lost.

In [12]:
KEEP = ["short_desc", "year", "reference_period_desc", "freq_desc",
        "begin_code", "end_code", "Value", "unit_desc", "class_desc", "domain_desc"]

def to_frame(rows, query_name):
    df = pd.DataFrame(rows)
    df = df[[col for col in KEEP if col in df.columns]].copy()
    df["value_raw"] = df["Value"].astype(str).str.strip()
    df["value"] = pd.to_numeric(df["value_raw"].str.replace(",", "", regex=False), errors="coerce")
    df["year"] = df["year"].astype(int)
    df["query"] = query_name
    return df.drop(columns="Value")

tables = {name: to_frame(rows, name) for name, rows in raw.items()}

What we're looking for: the exact names of the series we need:

def inventory_of(df):
    return (
        df.groupby(["short_desc", "freq_desc", "unit_desc"], dropna=False)
        .agg(periods=("reference_period_desc", lambda s: ", ".join(sorted(s.unique())[:6])
                      + (" ..." if s.nunique() > 6 else "")),
             first_year=("year", "min"), last_year=("year", "max"),
             rows=("value", "size"), numeric=("value", "count"))
        .reset_index()
    )

How to read it¶

The same for slaughter. Series names start with the class of animal (BULLS, COWS, HEIFERS, STEERS, CALVES), then the plant type: COMMERCIAL (all plants), FI (federally inspected plants only) or NFI (not federally inspected), then the unit (head, or pounds per head).

What it means¶

Reviewed together after the first run, and recorded as decision D25:

  • Both downloads are complete. Every value is numeric (no (D) codes), and every slaughter series covers all 201 months from January 2010 to September 2026.
  • Herd: we use the January 1 inventory, because NASS skipped its July report in 2013, 2016 and 2024, so January is the only complete yearly series. Three series: all cattle, beef cows, and beef replacement heifers (young females kept back to become cows; a rising count is the first sign of rebuilding).
  • Slaughter: three federally inspected (FI) series: beef cows (COWS, (EXCL MILK)), heifers and steers. FI is the only version NASS splits by cow, heifer and steer, and it covers almost all commercial slaughter. Heifers and steers together give the heifer share of slaughter, an industry signal: when it's high, young females are going to slaughter instead of being kept for breeding, so the herd is shrinking.

Step 9 · Slaughter table¶

One row per month. Two extra columns help read it:

  • heifer_share_pct = heifers ÷ (heifers + steers) × 100. Steers can't breed, so they are always slaughtered; heifers can either be slaughtered or kept to become cows. A higher share means more heifers are going to slaughter instead of into the herd.
  • beef_cows_slaughtered_12mo = beef cows slaughtered over the latest 12 months. Monthly slaughter has a strong seasonal pattern (more cows are culled in the fall, after weaning), so a rolling 12-month total shows the trend without the seasons.

How to read it¶

  • beef_cows_jan1_M / replacement_heifers_jan1_M: the herd on January 1, in millions.
  • beef_cows_slaughtered_M: beef cows slaughtered during the year, in millions.
  • cow_slaughter_pct_of_herd: that number as a share of the January 1 beef cow herd.
  • heifer_share_pct: heifers as a share of steer + heifer slaughter.
  • months: months of data (12 except 2026).

What it means¶

The sell-off: the share of the beef cow herd sent to slaughter climbed from about 7.6% in 2015 to 13.2% in 2022, the peak, which lines up with the worst drought year. In 2022 alone about 4 million beef cows were slaughtered.

Heifers went too: the heifer share of slaughter was at or near 40% from 2022 to 2024, its highest in this data. Ranchers weren't keeping young females to replace the cows they sold, which is why the herd kept shrinking after 2022.

Now: cow slaughter fell back to about 8.4% of the herd in 2025 and is lower again in 2026 so far. Ranchers have stopped selling off cows, but replacement heifers are still near their lowest, so the herd isn't growing back yet. A smaller herd for longer is consistent with beef prices staying high.

The earlier cycle, for comparison (D24): the 2011 drought produced a similar pattern, with 12.3% of the herd slaughtered in 2011 and the herd bottoming out in 2014. Ranchers then rebuilt: replacement heifers peaked in 2016–2017 and the herd grew back to its 2019 peak. The 2022 sell-off (13.2%) was bigger than 2011's, and this time the rebuild hasn't started.

This is the pattern D17 predicts. Whether drought came first is tested in item 1.6 (drought data) and Milestone 4 (item 4.4).

How to read it¶

  • Top: the beef cow herd (brown, left axis) and replacement heifers (blue, right axis) each January 1.
  • Middle: beef cows slaughtered each month (light bars) and the 12-month average (red line), which smooths out the fall culling season.

What it means¶

The order matches the D17 chain:

  1. Cow slaughter rose from 2019 and peaked in 2022, during the drought years.
  2. The herd fell from 2019 to 2026, fastest in 2022–2024, right after the peak sell-off.
  3. Ground beef prices climbed steadily from 2021, and fastest from 2024, as the herd reached its lowest point.
  4. Replacement heifers stayed low, so there's no rebuild yet to bring prices down.

One honest caveat: cow slaughter was already rising from about 2016, and replacement heifers started falling in 2018, before the drought. That's the normal cattle cycle turning after a rebuild. The drought didn't start the decline; it accelerated it, pushing cow slaughter to its 2022 peak and keeping heifers from being held back.

The 2020 price spike (COVID plant shutdowns) shows up in the bottom panel without any change in the herd: a separate, short-lived cause. The long climb from 2021 lines up with the herd. This is timing, not proof of cause; Milestone 4 measures the delays between each step.
