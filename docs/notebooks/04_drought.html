04 · Drought in cattle country¶

Milestone 1, item 1.6 · see docs/MILESTONES.md

What we're looking for¶

The first link in the D17 chain: drought → less grazing → cow sell-off → smaller herd → higher beef prices. Notebook 03 showed the sell-off and the shrinking herd; this notebook measures the drought, month by month, so we can check it came first.

Drought only matters for beef where the cattle are, so we build one number for "drought in cattle country", decided together (D26):

  1. States: the states that together hold about 75% of U.S. beef cows.
  2. Weights: each state counts in proportion to its beef cows on January 1, 2019, the herd's peak before the drought. Fixed weights keep a state that lost cows to drought from counting less afterwards, which would hide the drought.
  3. Measures:
    • % of area in severe drought or worse (D2–D4): the plain-English headline.
    • DSCI (Drought Severity and Coverage Index, 0–500): one number combining how much area is in drought and how severe it is. Used for the statistics in Milestone 4.
  4. Period: January 2010 onward, matching the herd data (D24), so the 2011 drought is included. Weekly maps are averaged to months.

The drought categories¶

The U.S. Drought Monitor classifies every part of the country each week:

Category Meaning
D0 Abnormally dry (not yet drought)
D1 Moderate drought
D2 Severe drought
D3 Extreme drought
D4 Exceptional drought

Step 2 · Beef cows by state, and the state weights¶

What we're looking for: each state's beef cows on January 1, 2019, ranked from most to least. We keep states, largest first, until they add up to at least 75% of the U.S. total. Each kept state's weight is its share of the kept states' cows, so the weights add to 1.

NASS sometimes reports small states together as an "OTHER STATES" row. That isn't a real state, so it's dropped before ranking.

WEIGHT_YEAR = 2019
COVERAGE_TARGET = 75   # percent of U.S. beef cows

resp = requests.get("https://quickstats.nass.usda.gov/api/api_GET/", params={
    "key": NASS_API_KEY, "format": "JSON",
    "short_desc": "CATTLE, COWS, BEEF - INVENTORY",
    "agg_level_desc": "STATE", "source_desc": "SURVEY",
    "reference_period_desc": "FIRST OF JAN", "year": str(WEIGHT_YEAR),
}, timeout=120)
if resp.status_code != 200:
    raise RuntimeError(f"NASS failed ({resp.status_code}): {resp.text[:300]}")
(RAW_DIR / "nass_beef_cows_by_state.json").write_text(resp.text)

states = pd.DataFrame(resp.json()["data"])
states = states[states["domain_desc"] == "TOTAL"].copy()
states["beef_cows"] = pd.to_numeric(states["Value"].str.replace(",", "", regex=False), errors="coerce")
us_total = states["beef_cows"].sum()   # U.S. total, including any grouped "other states"

# NASS sometimes adds an "OTHER STATES" row (FIPS 98/99) for small states it groups together;
# it isn't a real state, so it can't be matched to drought data
states = states[~states["state_name"].str.upper().str.startswith("OTHER")
                & ~states["state_fips_code"].astype(str).isin(["98", "99"])]
states = (states[["state_name", "state_alpha", "state_fips_code", "beef_cows"]]
          .dropna().sort_values("beef_cows", ascending=False).reset_index(drop=True))

states["share_of_us_pct"] = states["beef_cows"] / us_total * 100
states["cumulative_pct"] = states["share_of_us_pct"].cumsum()

n_keep = int((states["cumulative_pct"] < COVERAGE_TARGET).sum()) + 1
cattle_states = states.head(n_keep).copy()
cattle_states["weight"] = cattle_states["beef_cows"] / cattle_states["beef_cows"].sum()

print(f"{n_keep} states hold {cattle_states['share_of_us_pct'].sum():.1f}% of U.S. beef cows ({WEIGHT_YEAR})")
cattle_states.assign(beef_cows_M=cattle_states["beef_cows"] / 1e6)[
    ["state_name", "state_alpha", "state_fips_code", "beef_cows_M", "share_of_us_pct", "cumulative_pct", "weight"]
].round(3)

How to read it¶

One row per kept state: beef cows on January 1, 2019 (millions), its share of the U.S. total, the running total, and its weight in the cattle-country measure (the weights add up to 1).

What it means¶

  • It takes 17 states to reach 75% of U.S. beef cows (76.2%). Beef cows are spread widely: across the Plains, the Midwest, the South and the Mountain West.
  • Texas dominates: with about 4.7 million beef cows, it holds 14.7% of the U.S. total and gets a weight of 0.19, more than twice any other state. Drought in Texas moves this measure more than anything else, which matches how drought affects the national herd.
  • After Texas come Oklahoma, Missouri, Nebraska and South Dakota (about 6–7% of U.S. beef cows each), then 12 states between about 2% and 5%, ending with Virginia.

Step 3 · Download weekly drought statistics¶

For each state we ask the Drought Monitor for the weekly percent of the state's area in each category, using its traditional (cumulative) format: the D1 column means "D1 or worse", D2 means "D2 or worse", and so on. That makes D2 directly the "% in severe drought or worse" we want.

We also download the lower 48 states (CONUS) as a national comparison.

One request per state; each saved untouched to the dated raw folder.

USDM = "https://usdmdataservices.unl.edu/api"
START = "1/1/2010"
END = f"{date.today():%m/%d/%Y}"

def usdm_get(area, aoi):
    url = f"{USDM}/{area}/GetDroughtSeverityStatisticsByAreaPercent"
    params = {"aoi": aoi, "startdate": START, "enddate": END, "statisticsType": 1}
    resp = requests.get(url, params=params, headers={"Accept": "application/json"}, timeout=120)
    if resp.status_code != 200:
        raise RuntimeError(f"Drought Monitor failed for {aoi} ({resp.status_code}): {resp.text[:300]}")
    return resp.json()

Step 4 · Load and check¶

The service's field names can differ slightly in capitalization, so we lowercase them first. The week's date comes from validStart (the first day the map applies), falling back to mapDate.

Two checks:

  • In cumulative format each category includes the worse ones, so for every week D0 ≥ D1 ≥ D2 ≥ D3 ≥ D4 must hold. If it doesn't, the data came back in the other (categorical) format and we'd need to add the columns up instead.
  • All percentages should be between 0 and 100.
In [4]:
def to_weekly(rows, area):
    df = pd.DataFrame(rows)
    df.columns = [col.lower() for col in df.columns]
    date_col = "validstart" if "validstart" in df.columns else "mapdate"
    raw_dates = df[date_col].astype(str)
    if raw_dates.str.fullmatch(r"\d{8}").all():
        df["week"] = pd.to_datetime(raw_dates, format="%Y%m%d")
    else:
        df["week"] = pd.to_datetime(raw_dates.str[:10])
    for col in ["d0", "d1", "d2", "d3", "d4"]:
        df[col] = pd.to_numeric(df[col], errors="coerce")
    df["area"] = area
    return df[["area", "week", "d0", "d1", "d2", "d3", "d4"]].sort_values("week")

weekly = pd.concat([to_weekly(rows, area) for area, rows in drought_raw.items()], ignore_index=True)

cumulative_ok = ((weekly["d0"] >= weekly["d1"] - 0.01) & (weekly["d1"] >= weekly["d2"] - 0.01)
                 & (weekly["d2"] >= weekly["d3"] - 0.01) & (weekly["d3"] >= weekly["d4"] - 0.01)).mean() * 100
in_range = weekly[["d0", "d1", "d2", "d3", "d4"]].stack().between(0, 100).mean() * 100

print(f"Weeks where D0 >= D1 >= ... >= D4: {cumulative_ok:.1f}%  (should be 100)")
print(f"Values between 0 and 100:          {in_range:.1f}%  (should be 100)")
weekly.groupby("area")["week"].agg(["min", "max", "count"])

Step 5 · Monthly averages and the cattle-weighted measures¶

For each state and month:

  • d2_plus_pct: average % of the state's area in severe drought or worse (the D2 column).
  • dsci: the Drought Severity and Coverage Index, the sum of the five cumulative columns (D0 + D1 + D2 + D3 + D4). That's the same as weighting each category's area by 1 for D0 up to 5 for D4, which is how the Drought Monitor defines it. It runs from 0 (no dryness anywhere) to 500 (the whole state in exceptional drought).

Then the cattle-country value for each month is the weighted average across the kept states, using the 2019 beef cow weights from Step 2.

In [5]:
weekly["d2_plus_pct"] = weekly["d2"]
weekly["dsci"] = weekly[["d0", "d1", "d2", "d3", "d4"]].sum(axis=1)
weekly["month"] = weekly["week"].dt.to_period("M").dt.to_timestamp()
# The first map of 2010 starts on December 29, 2009; keep 2010 onward only
weekly = weekly[weekly["month"] >= "2010-01-01"]

monthly_state = weekly.groupby(["area", "month"])[["d2_plus_pct", "dsci"]].mean().reset_index()

weights = cattle_states.set_index("state_alpha")["weight"]
in_states = monthly_state[monthly_state["area"].isin(weights.index)].copy()
in_states["w"] = in_states["area"].map(weights)

def weighted(group, col):
    return (group[col] * group["w"]).sum() / group["w"].sum()

drought = pd.DataFrame({
    "cattle_d2_plus_pct": in_states.groupby("month").apply(weighted, "d2_plus_pct", include_groups=False),
    "cattle_dsci": in_states.groupby("month").apply(weighted, "dsci", include_groups=False),
})
conus = monthly_state[monthly_state["area"] == "CONUS"].set_index("month")
drought["conus_d2_plus_pct"] = conus["d2_plus_pct"]
drought["conus_dsci"] = conus["dsci"]
drought = drought.rename_axis("date")

yearly_drought = drought.groupby(drought.index.year).mean().round(1)

How to read it¶

Yearly averages of the monthly values:

  • cattle_d2_plus_pct: average % of cattle country in severe drought or worse.
  • cattle_dsci: the same, as the 0–500 index.
  • conus_…: the same measures for the lower 48 states as a whole, for comparison.

What it means¶

Two big droughts, and a third starting:

Drought Worst year (cattle country, % in severe drought or worse) Peak month
2011–2013 2012: 38.5% on average September 2012: 63%
2020–2023 2022: 34.9% on average late 2022: about 40% (quarterly average)
2026 so far 32.8% on average spring 2026: about 44% (quarterly average)
  • The 2020–2023 drought built up in stages: under 5% of cattle country in early 2020, about 20% from late 2020 through 2021, then 32–40% through 2022, easing in 2023 and mostly gone by 2024. That matches the "drought years" band we estimated in notebook 03.
  • Cattle country vs. the lower 48: in 2021 the drought was worse nationally (34%) than in cattle country (19%); it hit the West first. In 2022 cattle country caught up (about 35% for both), and in 2023 it stayed worse than the nation (24% vs 16%), as the drought settled over the Plains and Texas.
  • A new drought in 2026: severe drought returned to cattle country this year, reaching about 44% in the spring, as widespread as the 2022 peak. That matters for prices: it arrives while the herd is at its smallest and hasn't started rebuilding.

Step 6 · Chart: drought vs. cow slaughter¶

What we're looking for: the order of events. If drought drives the sell-off, the drought line should rise before or alongside beef cow slaughter (the 12-month average from notebook 03), and fall back before slaughter does.

How to read it¶

  • Shaded area (left axis): the % of cattle country in severe drought or worse, each month, weighted by beef cows.
  • Dashed line (left axis): the same measure for the whole lower 48, for comparison.
  • Red line (right axis): beef cows slaughtered per month, as a 12-month average. Each point averages the 12 months ending in that month, so the line runs about six months behind the events it reflects.

What it means¶

Both droughts were followed by a cow sell-off:

  • 2011–2013: cow slaughter was already high when the drought hit, stayed high through 2012, then fell steeply once the drought eased in 2014 and ranchers began rebuilding.
  • 2020–2023: drought spread from mid-2020; cow slaughter, which had been rising since 2016 with the normal cycle, climbed faster from 2021 and peaked in 2022, the worst drought year. When the drought eased in 2023–2024, cow slaughter fell back sharply.

So the order matches the D17 chain: drought first, sell-off following it, and the sell-off ending when the drought ends.

An open question for Milestone 4: the 2026 drought is as widespread as 2022's, yet cow slaughter has kept falling. One likely reason is that record cattle prices make holding on to cows (and buying hay) worth it this time; another is that the sell-off shows up with a delay. Item 4.4 will measure the lags properly and check this.

This is timing, not proof of cause, but it's the pattern the theory predicts in two separate droughts.
