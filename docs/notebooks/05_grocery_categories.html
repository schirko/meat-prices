05 · Grocery category prices (context)¶

Milestone 1, item 1.7 · see docs/MILESTONES.md

What we're looking for¶

Notebook 01 found that ground beef rose about 63% since 2015, while prices in general (the CPI) rose about 42%. This notebook answers the obvious follow-up: is beef unusual, or did all groceries go up this much? (decision D19)

We use Consumer Price Index (CPI) categories from BLS: one index for each part of the grocery cart. They aren't prices in dollars; each is an index that tracks how the cost of that category changes over time. To compare them, we rescale every series so its 2015 average = 100. A value of 140 then means "40% more expensive than in 2015".

Steps

  1. Setup and API key (same as notebook 01)
  2. The categories and their series IDs
  3. Download from BLS and save the raw response
  4. Tidy table and coverage check
  5. Rescale to 2015 = 100 and compare changes (from 2015 and 2019, D20)
  6. Chart: meat vs. the rest of the grocery cart
  7. Save the table

Step 5 · Rescale to 2015 = 100 and compare¶

First: what the BLS numbers are¶

A CPI value is an index level, not a price or a quantity. It has no units (no dollars, no pounds). BLS sets each index to 100 in a reference period, for most series the average of 1982–1984, and every later value compares the cost of the same basket of goods with that period. For example:

  • All items averaged 237.0 in 2015: the typical basket cost 2.37 times what it cost in 1982–84.
  • Beef and veal averaged 322.5 in 2015: beef cost 3.2 times its 1982–84 level.

Because each index measures change since its own reference period, two categories' levels can't be compared directly. Beef's 322.5 vs. all items' 237.0 doesn't mean beef is "more expensive"; it means beef rose more since 1982–84. Not every series even uses the same reference period: some categories BLS added later have a different one.

Rebasing: giving every category the same starting point¶

We rebase each series to our own starting point. For every month:

rescaled value = (this month's value ÷ that series' 2015 average) × 100

Worked example with the real values from this download:

All items Beef and veal
2015 average (BLS index level) 237.0 322.5
September 2026 (BLS index level) 336.9 about 512
Rescaled 2015 237.0 ÷ 237.0 × 100 = 100 322.5 ÷ 322.5 × 100 = 100
Rescaled September 2026 336.9 ÷ 237.0 × 100 = 142 512 ÷ 322.5 × 100 = 159
Meaning 42% more than 2015 59% more than 2015

After rebasing, every series starts at 100, so the lines can share one chart: anything above 100 is the percent increase since 2015 (159 → +59%), anything below is a decrease. The shape of each line doesn't change at all; only the starting point moves.

Why the 2015 average and not January 2015: a single month can be unusual; the average of all twelve months gives a steadier starting point (the same choice as in notebook 01).

In the code, avg(2015) calculates each series' 2015 average, .map(...) looks up the right average for each row by its series ID, and the row's value is divided by it and multiplied by 100.

Following D20 we show the change from both 2015 and 2019 (the last year before COVID), because the starting year changes the story.

In [5]:
avg = lambda year: cpi[cpi["date"].dt.year == year].groupby("series_id")["value"].mean()
cpi["index_2015"] = cpi["value"] / cpi["series_id"].map(avg(2015)) * 100

latest = cpi.dropna(subset=["value"]).sort_values("date").groupby("series_id").tail(1).set_index("series_id")
compare = pd.DataFrame({
    "group": latest["group"], "category": latest["category"],
    "latest_month": latest["date"].dt.strftime("%Y-%m"),
    "change_since_2015_pct": (latest["value"] / avg(2015) - 1) * 100,
    "change_since_2019_pct": (latest["value"] / avg(2019) - 1) * 100,
})
compare.sort_values("change_since_2015_pct", ascending=False).round(1).reset_index(drop=True)

How to read it¶

Every category, sorted from the biggest increase since 2015 to the smallest. All items is the yardstick: anything above it rose faster than prices in general; anything below rose slower. Food at home is the yardstick for groceries specifically.

What it means¶

Beef is at the top of the whole list. Since 2015, beef and veal rose about 59%, more than any grocery category and more than prices in general (42%). Since 2019 the gap is even wider: beef +67%, while no other grocery category passed about +40%.

Groceries as a whole didn't outrun inflation. Food at home rose about 33% since 2015, less than all items (42%); since 2019 the two are about even (33% vs 32%). So "food got expensive" mostly means "everything got expensive", except beef.

Other findings:

  • Eating out rose much more than cooking at home: food away from home is up about 56% since 2015 vs. 33% for groceries. (Wages are a large part of restaurant costs.)
  • Packaged foods ("other food at home", +35%) and canned and frozen produce (+35%) rose a little more than groceries overall; fresh produce (+24%) and dairy (+21%) rose less.
  • Pork (+26%) and poultry (+34%) rose less than all items, matching notebook 01. (Pork's CPI rose more than the pork-chop price in notebook 01, +9%, because the CPI covers every pork product, including bacon and ham.)
  • Eggs show why the baseline matters (D20): −2% since 2015 but +37% since 2019. 2015 was itself a bird-flu year with very high egg prices, so it's a misleading starting point for eggs.

How to read it¶

Every line starts near 100 in 2015. A line at 150 means that category costs 50% more than in 2015. Beef is the thick red line; the dashed black and gray lines are food at home and all items. Eggs are dotted because their spikes (bird flu) would otherwise dominate the chart. The break in every line in late 2025 is the missing October 2025.

What it means¶

Yearly averages of the rebased values tell the story of the chart:

Year Beef and veal Food at home All items Food away from home
2019 95 100 108 111
2021 114 107 114 120
2023 125 125 129 138
2025 146 129 136 149
  • Until 2019, beef was actually cheaper than in 2015 (95), while prices in general had already risen 8%.
  • In 2020–2021, beef jumped (COVID plant shutdowns, then rising demand) and caught up with all items.
  • In 2022–2023, everything rose fast (general inflation): groceries, restaurants and beef moved together.
  • From 2024, beef broke away on its own, rising from about 125 to 146 in two years while groceries barely moved (125 to 129). That's when the herd hit its low point (notebook 03): the beef-specific cause shows up exactly when beef separates from everything else.
