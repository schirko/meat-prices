1 · Prices at the time vs. adjusted for inflation¶

What we're looking for: how much of each meat's price rise is just general inflation. The solid line is the price as it was each month; the dashed line is the same price in today's dollars (D27). How to read it¶

Each panel is one meat. Solid: the price as shoppers paid it. Dashed: that same price in today's dollars. If the dashed line falls over time, the meat got cheaper compared with everything else; if it rises, it outpaced inflation.

What it means¶

  • Beef (in today's dollars) fell from about $6.10/lb in early 2015 to about $5.00 in 2017–2019, spiked in 2020 (COVID plant shutdowns), sat around $5.50 in 2021–2023, then climbed to about **$6.80–7.00** in 2025–2026, now above its early-2015 level. Most of beef's real rise happened after 2023.
  • Pork fell almost steadily in real terms, from about $5.70 to $4.20, apart from the 2020 spike. It got cheaper compared with everything else.
  • Chicken breast fell to about $4.00 by 2019, spiked to about $5.40 in 2022, then slid back to about $4.20, below its 2015 level in real terms.

How to read it¶

Four panels, one timeline (2010 to now; the price panel starts in 2015). The herd is drawn as steps because it's counted once a year. The slaughter line is a 12-month total, so it reacts about six months after a change in monthly slaughter.

What it means¶

The chain shows up twice:

  • The earlier cycle: drought in 2011–2013 → cow slaughter already high in 2011, then falling as the herd ran low → the herd bottomed out in 2014 → ranchers rebuilt through 2019. Beef prices were high in real terms in 2015 and fell through 2019 as the herd grew back. So the price panel starts at the tail end of the previous cycle.
  • The recent cycle: drought from mid-2020, worst in 2022 → cow slaughter peaked in 2022 → the herd fell in steps from 2020 to its 2026 low → the real beef price rose slowly in 2021–2023 and fastest in 2024–2026, as the herd reached its low.

The order matches D17: drought, then sell-off, then a smaller herd, then higher real prices, with each step lagging the one before.

4 · A first look at the delays¶

What we're looking for: roughly how long each step takes to follow the one before.

A simple way to look: shift one series later by 0, 1, 2… months (or years) and see how closely it then moves with the other, measured by the correlation (−1 to 1; near 1 means they rise and fall together). The shift with the strongest correlation hints at the delay.

An important caution: two series that both trend upward will look correlated even if they're unrelated. To avoid that, we compare year-over-year changes (this month vs. the same month a year earlier), not the levels. That also removes seasonal patterns. Even so, this is only a first look; Milestone 4 tests the delays properly.

A. Drought → cow slaughter (yearly): each year's average drought (DSCI) vs. that year's beef cow slaughter as a % of the January 1 herd, with slaughter shifted 0–2 years later. We use yearly values here because monthly slaughter is noisy and seasonal, and a cow sell-off plays out over a year rather than a month. (A first try with monthly year-over-year changes gave no clear signal for exactly that reason.) The slaughter rate is a level, not a change, but it doesn't trend steadily upward, so the caution above matters less here. There are 16 years (2010–2025), so again: a hint, not a measurement.

How to read it¶

Each dot is one year: how much drought cattle country had (left to right) and what share of the beef cow herd was slaughtered (bottom to top). If drought drives the sell-off, the dots should rise from left to right. The printed correlations show how the relationship changes when slaughter is compared with drought one and two years earlier.

What it means¶

  • Same year: correlation +0.58. Years with more drought had more beef cows slaughtered: 2011, 2022 and 2023 sit at the top right. That's the expected direction.

  • Two years later: −0.39. Two years after a drought, cow slaughter tends to be lower: fewer cows are left to sell, and ranchers start keeping them to rebuild.

  • Exceptions worth explaining in Milestone 4:

    • 2012–2013 had heavy drought but falling slaughter: much of the herd had already been sold in 2011.
    • 2024–2025 had moderate drought but low slaughter: with record cattle prices, holding on to cows paid off.

    Drought alone doesn't decide the sell-off; how many cows are left, and what cattle are worth, matter too.

B. Herd → beef price (yearly): the year-over-year change in the January 1 beef cow herd vs. the year-over-year change in the real beef price (yearly averages), with the price shifted 0–3 years later.

There are only about ten years of overlap (prices start in 2015), so treat this as a hint, not a measurement.

6 · Seasonality¶

What we're looking for: whether prices follow a regular yearly pattern, for example higher in grilling season. If they do, the app's single-year view (D11) should mention it, so a summer rise isn't mistaken for an event.

For each month we compare the price with the average of the surrounding 12 months (a centered 12-month average), then average that difference for each calendar month across 2015–2026. A bar of +2% means that month is typically 2% above the yearly trend.

In [8]:
season = {}
for meat, label in MEATS.items():
    s = prices[f"{meat}_price"]
    trend = s.rolling(12, center=True).mean()
    dev = (s / trend - 1) * 100
    season[label] = dev.groupby(dev.index.month).mean()
season = pd.DataFrame(season)
season.index = pd.to_datetime(season.index, format="%m").strftime("%b")

ax = season.plot.bar(figsize=(11, 4), color=[COLORS[m] for m in MEATS], width=0.8)
ax.axhline(0, color="#888888", linewidth=0.8)
ax.set_ylabel("% above / below the 12-month trend")
ax.set_title("Typical seasonal pattern, 2015-2026", loc="left", fontweight="bold")
ax.grid(alpha=0.3, axis="y")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

How to read it¶

The solid line is the chosen year, the dashed gray line the year before. The two printed lines give the yearly averages, the change, and the lowest and highest months.

What it means¶

For 2021, ground beef averaged **$4.33/lb, up 5.2% on 2020**. It rose steadily all year, from $3.96 in January to $4.72 in October. The 2020 line shows the COVID spike (May–June 2020) and how quickly it reversed. The jump from May to June 2021 lines up with the JBS cyberattack, one of the events Milestone 3 will test.

This layout works for the app: one year, the year before as a reference, and a one-line summary.

Questions for Milestone 4¶

From what the charts show, these are the questions worth testing properly:

  1. Beef vs. inflation: how large is beef's real price rise since 2015 and 2019, and how confident can we be that it's different from pork and chicken? (items 4.1, 4.5)
  2. Drought → sell-off: how much does drought raise cow slaughter, once we account for how many cows are left and what cattle are worth? Can that explain 2012–2013 and 2024–2025? (item 4.4)
  3. Herd → price: how much does the real beef price rise for each 1% fall in the herd, and over what delay (the charts suggest 0–1 year)? (item 4.4)
  4. Events: did the price really shift at the 2020 plant shutdowns, the June 2021 JBS cyberattack, the 2022 chicken spike and beef's 2024 break-away? (items 4.3 and Milestone 3)
  5. Who gets the dollar: is the store's larger real spread since 2022 a lasting change or a return to 2015 levels? (item 4.2)
  6. The 2026 drought: it's as widespread as 2022's, but cow slaughter keeps falling. Worth watching, and worth a note in the app.

What's next and why¶

Milestone 3: the event timeline. Several of these questions depend on when things happened: the plant shutdowns, the JBS cyberattack, import suspensions, screwworm, and the popular explanations we agreed to test (the 2022 plant fires and the Colorado culls).
