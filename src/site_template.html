Key findings

  • Beef is the outlier. Ground beef is up ${beef_nominal}% since 2015, while prices in general rose ${all_prices}% and groceries ${groceries}%. After inflation, beef costs ${beef_real}% more; pork chops cost ${pork_real}% less and chicken breast ${chicken_real}% less.
  • The cause is a cattle shortage, and drought started it. Drought across cattle country pushed ranchers to sell off cows. The sell-off peaked in ${selloff_year}, when ${selloff_pct}% of the beef cow herd was slaughtered in one year, and the herd shrank ${herd_change}% from its ${herd_peak_year} peak. Fewer cows means fewer calves and less beef for years. Measured link by link, a 1% smaller herd goes with about a 1.8% higher real beef price, so the shrinking herd accounts for almost all of beef's real rise since 2019. Drought explains roughly 40% of the herd loss; ranchers' other choices explain the rest.
  • Sudden events explain little of beef's rise. Of 16 dated events, only the 2020 COVID plant closures clearly shifted all three meats (11–17% above where prices were heading, then fading within months). For beef, plant fires and closures, the JBS cyberattack and screwworm show no clear shift.
  • The popular explanations don't hold up. Plant fires, Colorado's bird-flu culls and the cage-free egg law came at times when prices didn't shift. Chicken's 2022 spike was real, but it lines up with feed costs and bird flu.
  • Ranchers are getting a bigger share of the money. Their share of each retail beef dollar rose from ${rancher_2019}% in 2019 to ${rancher_latest}% in ${rancher_year}, which points to scarce cattle rather than wider packer or store margins.

How it was done

  1. Collected public data from four sources: retail prices and inflation (BLS), price spreads and cattle counts (USDA ERS and NASS), and drought (U.S. Drought Monitor).
  2. Cleaned and combined everything into one monthly table from 2010 on, documenting every fix, including problems found in the official files.
  3. Explored and tested. Patterns were charted, 16 events were dated and sourced, and each event was tested by forecasting where prices were heading and checking whether the gap was bigger than in ordinary months.
  4. Automated. One command re-downloads, rebuilds and checks the data, and automated tests run on every change.

Limits. These are national averages; local prices differ. October 2025 is estimated because the government shutdown stopped price collection that month. Overlapping events can't be told apart. Few years of data (12–16 per link) limit how precise the measured links can be. A final verdict is still in progress.
